# Base-paper reproduction: results summary

Generated by `tools/basepaper/make_summary.py` from the saved runs in this directory (5 runs per point; machine and commit in `machine.txt`). Nothing here was tuned to match the paper; where we differ from it, that is the finding. The paper's dataset is not available, so accuracy is compared by trend only.

Machine: 13th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-13650HX, 14 physical cores (a hybrid CPU: 6 performance cores with SMT, then 8 efficiency cores), Linux, gcc 15.2.0, `-O2`. Commit 3358f98dffa6.

## Claims of the base paper

Claim numbers follow `docs/research/literature_review.md` §3. Values are medians over runs with [min..max] in brackets.

| # | Claim (paper) | Paper's figure | Our measurement | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Parallel regex matchers speed up classification (§1, §3.3) | 2.4× over sequential | `fork` (the paper's design) vs sequential at 10,000 inputs: 0.08× vs `seq-recompile`, 0.0024× vs `seq-precompiled`. `threads-pattern`: 0.09× vs `seq-precompiled`. `threads-data` (5 threads): 3.1×; `dfa` (5 threads): 25× | **Not reproduced** for per-pattern parallelism; supported for data parallelism and the combined DFA | Splitting one input's five patterns over processes or threads costs more than the matching it saves (five `regexec` calls on a precompiled set take under a microsecond). Parallelism pays when it splits the inputs, and most when one DFA checks all patterns in one pass. |
| 2 | Per-input process creation limits small batches; ~10+ inputs work better (§5.3) | speedup needs batches above ~10 | `fork` inputs/s: batch 1 2341 [1642..4165], batch 10 4788 [4754..4900], batch 10,000 4691 [4539..4698]; never above `seq-recompile` (5.911e+04 [5.864e+04..5.977e+04] at 10,000) | **Partly reproduced** | Throughput rises 2.0× from 1 to 10 inputs, then stays flat, but `fork` is below both sequential baselines at every batch size. The cost is paid per input (five forks each), so a bigger batch cannot amortise it. |
| 3 | Near-linear scaling to 6 cores, 5.62× at 8 (§4.3) | 1.92× at 2, 3.48× at 4, 4.87× at 6, 5.62× at 8 | `fork` speedup vs itself on 1 core: 2: 1.17, 4: 1.70, 6: 1.65, 8: 1.72; `threads-pattern`: 2: 1.09, 4: 1.50, 6: 2.53, 8: 2.54; `threads-data`: 2: 1.96, 4: 3.44, 6: 4.83, 8: 4.48 (cores in the order 2, 4, 6, 8) | **Not reproduced** for the paper's design; reproduced in shape for data parallelism | Pattern parallelism cannot exceed 5× with 5 patterns, and `fork` peaks at 1.72× and `threads-pattern` at 2.54×. Splitting the inputs scales near-linearly to 4–6 cores (`threads-data` 4.83× at 6); at 8 it drops because cores 7 and 8 here are slower efficiency cores. Only `dfa` (a data-parallel variant) exceeds 5×: dfa at 6 cores = 5.29×. |
| 4 | Structured formats are classified more accurately than natural-language ones (§4.2) | email/phone 98.7%, questions/greetings 93.2% | Per-label decision accuracy on our 1,250-line labeled set, no normalisation: EMAIL+PHONE 0.979, QUESTION+GREETING 0.952. With the paper's input module: 0.935 vs 0.958 | **Qualitatively supported** (as typed); reversed with the paper's input module | Without normalisation the order matches the paper (EMAIL F1 0.994 vs QUESTION 0.871 and GREETING 0.827), though PHONE (0.886) is close to them. The paper's character whitelist rejects 146 of the 1250 lines (for example `+`, `'`) and lowercasing makes the NAME pattern impossible (0 true positives), which drops EMAIL and PHONE recall. Absolute values depend on our templates. |
| 5 | Single-winner conflict resolution, weights 0.4/0.3/0.3, confidence score (§3.4) | 85–98% confidence in the §4.4 table | Not run (R5 not done). The case it matters for is common here: 120 of 1250 labeled lines truly belong to 2+ categories, and the five patterns fire together on 120 lines | **Not tested** | A single winner would discard the other labels on those lines. The paper's scoring functions are undefined, so reproducing the §4.4 table needs the optional task R5. |
| 6 | 8.9 ms sequential vs 3.7 ms parallel for a short input (§4.1) | 8.9 ms / 3.7 ms for `john@example.com` | `fork` per-input latency: 0.193 [0.191..0.194] ms for `john@example.com`, 0.191–0.208 ms across the five inputs; `seq-recompile`: 0.0151–0.0171 ms; `seq-precompiled`: 0.00029–0.0007 ms | **Not reproduced** | Our `fork` version is 14–19 times faster than the paper's parallel time for the same input (different machine), but 12–13× slower than the recompiling sequential loop it was meant to beat (paper: 2.4× faster). The paper's hypothesis that process overhead dominates holds: in each child `regcomp` takes about 20 µs and `regexec` about 3 µs, out of 203 µs from first fork to last `waitpid`. |

## How this was produced

```
make bp                                   # bin/bp_fork, bin/bp_bench
tools/basepaper/run_bp.sh --full           # raw JSON, machine.txt, summaries, figures
python3 tools/basepaper/make_summary.py    # this file
```

Figures: `figures/rb1.svg` … `rb5.svg`, `rb3_vs_seq.svg`. Raw runs: `rb1/` … `rb5/`. Inputs: `tests/basepaper/` (`table41.txt`, `labeled.tsv`, generated by `tools/basepaper/gen_bp.py`). Variants are described in `docs/project_design.md` §10.4.

Limits: one machine, one hybrid CPU; the paper's dataset and machine (i7-10750H) are not available; the paper's pattern list is reproduced verbatim but its input text for timings is a handful of strings. Interpretations we had to choose (character-list reading of `ALLOWED_CHARS`, pinning inside the allowed CPU set, wording of the per-core output lines) are in `docs/progress.md`.
